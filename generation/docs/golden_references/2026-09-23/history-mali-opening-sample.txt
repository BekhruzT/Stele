A king from Mali spent so much gold in Cairo that people were still discussing its effect on prices years later.

In 1324, Mansa Musa passed through Egypt on his pilgrimage to Mecca. He brought a large entourage and substantial quantities of gold. The journey gave people in Cairo a direct encounter with the wealth of a West African kingdom, but it also brought two rulers into a delicate question of rank.

Musa was a king arriving in another king's capital. The Egyptian sultan, al-Nasir Muhammad, expected to receive him. Musa said he had come for the pilgrimage and did not wish to mix other business with it.

The account of their meeting comes from the writer al-Umari, who visited Cairo some years afterward and asked people about Musa's stay. One of his informants was a court official who had helped arrange the audience. We are hearing that official's recollection through a later account, rather than a transcript made in the room.

According to the official, the difficulty lay in the greeting. Court protocol required a gesture that would place Musa in a posture of submission before the sultan. Bringing valuable gifts was one thing. Accepting that position in front of another ruler was another.

The official kept pressing for the visit, and Musa eventually agreed to attend. When asked to bow to the ground, he initially refused. Then a member of his party spoke quietly to him. The account does not preserve what the adviser said. It records Musa declaring that his gesture was directed to God, before he lowered himself and approached the sultan.

That allowed the meeting to continue. The sultan welcomed him and seated him nearby. They spoke at length, and gifts followed: ceremonial clothing, horses, and provisions of honour for Musa and members of his party.

The episode shows wealth operating inside a relationship rather than outside it. Musa could bring gold across the Sahara, yet he still had to decide how to appear at another court. The host could insist on ceremony, yet he also received a visiting ruler whose generosity people would remember. The exchange involved both resources and recognition.

Al-Umari's account then follows the gold beyond the palace. Musa gave gifts to officials, and his party bought goods in the city. Cairo's merchants had customers able and willing to spend on an exceptional scale.

For the people receiving the gold, that could be profitable. For the gold itself, entering the market in larger quantities could change what it would buy. Its value was a relationship between the metal and other things available in exchange, not a number permanently attached to it.

Al-Umari reported that a standard weight of gold had previously fetched at least twenty-five silver dirhams, a unit of currency. In the period he described afterward, it fetched about twenty-two or less. The comparison is between gold and silver. It is not a record showing that every price in Cairo moved by the same amount.

He attributed the change to the gold brought and spent by Musa's party and said its effect was still visible roughly twelve years later. That is a striking contemporary interpretation. It does not give us a complete sequence of market prices, or prove that a single visit explains every change over the intervening years.

We can keep the interesting part without making it larger than the evidence. The visit was remembered for extraordinary spending, and an informed writer linked it to a lasting fall in gold's exchange value. We do not need an exact count of every camel or attendant to understand why the visit mattered.

The gold had reached Cairo through a much larger set of connections. Mali belonged to a world of towns, mining regions, markets, and caravan routes extending across the Sahara. Royal wealth could concentrate some of what moved through those connections. The pilgrimage made that concentration unusually visible in one place.

To see the ordinary work behind such a journey, we can turn to a traveller who crossed the desert nearly three decades later, going toward Mali rather than away from it.

In 1352, the Moroccan traveller Ibn Battuta set out from Sijilmasa, on the northern side of the Sahara. His preparations included camels and a substantial supply of fodder. Animals that made the crossing possible also had to be fed throughout it. The ability to carry a load was only one part of arranging its movement.

His account takes us to Taghaza, a salt-mining settlement. He described buildings made from blocks of salt and roofs of camel skins. There were no nearby fields supplying everything the inhabitants needed. Food arrived through the trade in which the mine itself took part.

The labour was not freely shared. Ibn Battuta identified the salt workers as enslaved people belonging to the Massufa, a Saharan community. Their work belongs in the account of the trade, alongside the merchants who carried the slabs and the rulers who drew wealth from commerce.

Salt was useful far from the place where it was dug. Ibn Battuta recorded that a camel-load fetched more at markets farther south than at the desert's edge. Transport, scarcity, and the circumstances of particular markets changed its price along the route. His account does not require the familiar claim that salt everywhere traded for an equal weight of gold.

A load that became more valuable farther away still had to get there. Before one difficult stage of the crossing, the caravan stopped to rest and repair its waterskins. The travellers filled them and added protective coverings. Their attention was on what would keep people and animals moving during the next days.

They also sent a messenger ahead to Walata, a town at the desert's southern edge. He carried letters asking contacts there to prepare lodgings and bring water out to meet the approaching caravan. A traveller without a personal friend could write to a merchant known for helping others.

This was an arrangement made through people already living at the destination. The travellers did not have to carry every drop for the whole distance if others could meet them. But that help depended on the message arriving, the request being understood, and someone making the journey outward with the supplies.

Ibn Battuta recalled seeing the fires of the party coming to meet them after days of travel. The description gives us a small, practical measure of a functioning connection. The letters had gone ahead. People had acted on them. The caravan could finish the approach with the help it had arranged.

At Walata, the work changed again. Goods were set down and guarded while merchants went to meet the local governor. A host had arranged accommodation for Ibn Battuta. After the open desert came a town with officials, households, and rules the visitors had to navigate.

Musa's gold could make an extraordinary impression in Cairo. The routes behind that impression depended on much less conspicuous exchanges: provisions for an animal, a place to store goods, a letter asking someone to bring water. Those were the connections a visitor encountered on the way into Mali, before he reached its royal court.
